Market data platform

A walkthrough of the kind of work behind the market data platform I built at EQ Risk Management Consulting. Eight panels, from a raw tick to a volatility surface to a backtest that mostly says no.

What this is, and what it is not

This is not the platform. The production system is proprietary and stays that way. Nothing on this page touches it. There is no connection to the firm’s data, no client information, and none of the logic that decides anything.

What you are looking at is a visual analogue, built standalone for this page. Every number is generated in your browser from published textbook models and a seeded random number generator. It is similar in nature to the real thing and a considerably lesser version of it: the shapes, the vocabulary and the questions are the same, the substance underneath is not.

Treat it as an illustration of what the work involves. Do not treat any number on this page as a result, and do not trade on it.

01

Where the data comes from

Everything downstream is only as good as the capture. A quote arrives over a WebSocket, lands in an in-memory buffer, and is written to a date-partitioned time-series store. Bars are derived from that, never fetched.

02

A bar being built, one tick at a time

The rightmost candle is still forming. It is drawn differently on purpose. A partial bar that looks like a finished one is the single easiest way to put lookahead bias into a backtest, because the high and low of a bar are not knowable until it closes.

03

The volatility surface

An option’s price is usually quoted as an implied volatility, the number you put into Black-Scholes to recover the market price. Plot that across every strike and every expiry and you get a surface. It is the single most useful picture in options because it says what the market expects, not what already happened.

How to read it

The floor is the money
One axis is strike relative to the forward, the other is time to expiry. The middle of the strike axis is at the money, which is where most of the volume is.
The tilt is the skew
Slice at one expiry and the curve is almost never flat. Downside strikes carry higher volatility than upside ones, because crashes are faster than rallies and the people hedging them are price takers.
The slope away from you is the term structure
Slice at one strike instead and you get volatility against time. In a calm market it rises with maturity. Before an event it inverts, with the near expiry the most expensive thing on the board.
What a trader actually does with it
Price something that is not quoted, by interpolating. Find something quoted away from the surface, which is either an opportunity or bad data, and it is bad data far more often. Hedge a book by knowing how the whole sheet moves when one number does.
Why it has to be fitted, not just plotted
Raw quotes are noisy, arrive at different times, and disagree. A fit gives one continuous surface you can differentiate, which is the next panel.

04

Fitting one expiry: the smile

A single slice of that surface, fitted with SVI, a five-parameter form that is standard in the literature and cheap to evaluate. The dots are quotes. The line is the fit. The panel underneath is what the fit missed, which is the part worth looking at.

05

Skew, tracked over time

Once every expiry is fitted every day, the fit parameters become a time series of their own. This is the cost of downside protection relative to upside, by maturity, on three days chosen to look different.

06

A backtest, gross and net

The gap between these two lines is the entire reason the cost model exists. Gross of costs, a mediocre strategy looks like a good one. Net of a calibrated slippage estimate, most of them stop existing.

07

The result that matters: mostly no

Fourteen candidate signal families, each tested out of sample against a matched baseline on identical bars and identical cost assumptions. Thirteen were statistically indistinguishable from the baseline and were retired.

08

How many independent bets you actually have

Counting trades overstates how much evidence you have, because the trades are correlated. Names in the same sector move together, and the same signal fires across them on the same day. Adjusting for that collapses the count, and the collapsed number is the one that governs how sure you can be.

What this page is doing

The point of the real platform is not that it draws nice pictures. It is that a number coming out of it can be trusted, which takes point-in-time reconstruction, cost modelling, and an evaluation regime willing to return a negative answer. The picture is the easy part and it is the part that fits on a web page.
