Market data platform

A walkthrough of the kind of work behind the market data platform I built at EQ Risk Management Consulting. Nine panels, from a raw tick to a volatility surface to a worked trade priced off it, by way of a backtest that mostly says no.

What this is, and what it is not

This is not the platform. The production system is proprietary and stays that way. Nothing on this page touches it. There is no connection to the firm’s data, no client information, and none of the logic that decides anything.

What you are looking at is a visual analogue, built standalone for this page. Every number is generated in your browser from published textbook models and a seeded random number generator. It is similar in nature to the real thing and a considerably lesser version of it: the shapes, the vocabulary and the questions are the same, the substance underneath is not.

Treat it as an illustration of what the work involves. Do not treat any number on this page as a result, and do not trade on it.

01

Where the data comes from

A quote arrives over a WebSocket, lands in an in-memory buffer, and is written to a date-partitioned store. Bars are derived from it rather than fetched.

I assumed getting the data would be the easy part and the models would be the hard part. It has been the other way round for most of this project.

What we tried first

Polling the vendor’s REST endpoint, because it took an afternoon to stand up. Measured against one liquid name over a single session it accounted for 13 to 15 percent of the shares that actually traded. The rest happened between polls. Everything live moved onto the stream after that, and the buffer was written so that only the fetch function had to change.

Two things cost real time. Our credential has no per-trade entitlement, so the trade feed is declined rather than delivered, and our own code logged that it had subscribed anyway because it never checked the response code on a subscribe. Separately, the stream sends delta frames that omit unchanged fields, and treating an absent field as zero quietly zeroed the volume on every frame that did not carry it.

02

A bar being built, one tick at a time

The rightmost candle is still forming, and is drawn differently for that reason. A bar’s high and low are not knowable until it closes.

Watching one bar build is what made lookahead bias concrete for me. I had read the definition plenty of times before that and it had not landed.

What we tried first

Re-fetching the current bar from the vendor on a timer. It lagged, and at the bucket boundary the same bar could arrive twice with different contents. The bar is now assembled in memory from the stream and anchored to the clock rather than to the arrival of the first tick.

We also tried publishing a provisional signal on the forming bar, and removed it. The pattern being detected needs bars on both sides to confirm, so it cannot be evaluated on the newest bar at all, and the flag could provably never return a row.

03

The volatility surface

An option’s price is usually quoted as an implied volatility, the number that puts Black-Scholes back onto the market price. Plot it across every strike and every expiry and you get a surface.

I understood volatility before I started this. I had no idea you could build a whole surface out of it, or that the surface would end up being the most useful thing on the screen.

Pick a name and a date. The five dates are months apart. Everything below, including the worked trade, reprices off the selection.

How to read it

The floor is the money
Strike relative to the forward on one axis, expiry on the other, volatility as height. The expiry axis carries dates rather than tenors, worked forward from the as-of date, because the board is a set of listed expirations and not a continuum.
The tilt is the skew
Slice at one expiry and the curve is almost never flat. Downside strikes carry higher volatility than upside ones.
The slope away from you is the term structure
Slice at one strike instead and you get volatility against time. It usually rises with maturity. Before an event it inverts, and the near expiry becomes the most expensive thing on the board. Compare the January and July dates above.
What a trader actually does with it
Three things. Price something that is not quoted, by interpolating. Find something quoted away from the surface, which is usually bad data and occasionally not. Hedge a book, which needs to know how the whole sheet moves when one number does.
Why it has to be fitted
Raw quotes are noisy, arrive at different times, and disagree. A fit gives one continuous surface you can differentiate.
The sheet is smooth, the market is not
Switch to quotes only and the continuum disappears. What is listed is fifteen expiries on discrete strikes, with holes in the wings. Everything between the slices is interpolation.

Both checks are recomputed on every market change rather than asserted once.

What broke first

Short maturities. Below about forty-five days the fitted at-the-money volatility collapsed on some dates, with the first percentile landing at 0.6 percent on an index that has never been that quiet. Those slices are now excluded rather than patched, because a fit that is wrong in a plausible-looking way is worse than a gap.

Building this page reproduced the same class of problem. The first parameterization of the model drawn here failed the butterfly check below, on the upside wing at short maturities, for exactly the reason the real fits did.

04

Fitting one expiry: the smile

The slice marked in blue on the surface above, fitted with SVI. Five parameters, standard in the literature, cheap enough to refit every expiry on every symbol overnight. All fifteen listed expiries are here, and picking one moves the line up there too. Each vertical mark is one option’s market drawn from its bid to its ask. The line is the fit, and it runs across the whole range including the stretches where nothing is listed, which is where it is least constrained and most likely to be wrong. The panel underneath is the residual.

What we tried first

Fitting whatever the history handed us. It contains quotes with non-positive implied volatility, and a fit that runs through them drags the whole smile down. They are dropped now, and the number dropped is reported rather than quietly absorbed.

05

Skew, tracked over time

Fit every expiry every day and the parameters become a time series of their own. This one is the cost of downside protection relative to upside.

What we got wrong

Anchoring the surface on the cash close. The at-the-money point belongs at the forward, not at spot, and the difference is financing and dividends rather than rounding. It is implied from put-call parity across every strike now and taken as a median, which survives the handful of strikes where one side of the market is stale.

06

A backtest, gross and net

The same signal on the same bars, before and after a calibrated slippage estimate.

The change that made everything look worse

Putting an embargo between the training and testing windows, so a feature with a fifty-bar lookback cannot see across the boundary. Out-of-sample performance fell on every symbol we ran it on. On one name it fell by more than the gap between any two of the feature sets we had been comparing, which means those comparisons had been reading differences smaller than the bias they shared.

07

The result that matters: mostly no

Fourteen candidate signal families, each tested out of sample against a matched baseline on identical bars and identical cost assumptions. Thirteen came back indistinguishable from the baseline and were retired.

This is not the answer I was hoping for. I spent a while convinced the harness was broken before I accepted that the harness was fine.

What we built to fix this, and what happened

Two feature families, specifically to give the model something it did not already have. One described the time of day and the position in the week. The other described the shape of the volatility surface, which was the only input we had that says what the market expects rather than what it already did.

In-sample fit rose steadily as we added them. Out of sample it fell, on every symbol, every time. An earlier walk-forward had said the same thing in a different way, when the configuration that ranked first in sample came 103rd of 144 out of sample.

08

How many independent bets you actually have

Counting trades overstates the evidence, because the trades are correlated. Names in the same sector move together, and the same signal fires across them on the same day.

When I first saw the adjusted number I assumed I had dropped three orders of magnitude somewhere in the code.

What we were counting before

Trades. For longer than we should have been. The thing that changed the research plan was not a new signal, it was noticing that the ones already in hand were largely the same bet placed again.

09

Using the surface: a worked trade, with the arithmetic

A three-month risk reversal, priced off the selected surface, step by step, and then costed. Change the underlying or the date above and every number here moves.

I could not have priced any of this before this project started. Working a trade through by hand, in this order, is how I came to understand what the surface is actually for.

Where the obvious test gave the wrong answer

One of the signal functions had an ambiguous direction, and the natural way to settle it was to measure the return from the bar where the pattern confirmed. That test said the labels were inverted, significantly, across twenty-six thousand signals.

It also changed sign between the first and second halves of the history, and it was measuring an entry the rule never described. Tested as the breakout it actually is, meaning the trade only exists if price trades through the level, the sign held in both halves.

What this page is doing

Drawing the surface is the easy part, and it is the part that fits on a web page. The work behind the real platform is point-in-time reconstruction, cost modelling, and an evaluation regime willing to come back negative.
