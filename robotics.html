Robot learning, and the gap

Two robots passing an object between them, in orbit, with nobody in the loop to correct a bad grasp. This is how the policy gets built, how it gets onto the arm, and what goes wrong in between.

About the figures

The numbers are measured. Where a figure is quoted in the text, it came out of the work. Frame counts, demonstration counts, latencies, error magnitudes and the results of the comparisons are all real.

The pictures are reconstructions. The rollouts, the curves and the trajectories on this page are generated in your browser to show the mechanism, because the lab’s logs and video are not mine to publish. Where a figure illustrates rather than reports, it says so.

01

Why handover is the hard case

One robot holds an object. Another has to take it. On the ground you can retry, and a person can step in. In orbit you cannot do either, and a failed grasp can put the object somewhere nobody can reach.

I came into this expecting the difficulty to be in the policy. Most of my time has gone into everything around it.

What I assumed going in

That the research question was which architecture to use. It turned out the architecture was rarely the thing standing between a policy that works in evaluation and one that works on the arm. Frame conventions, stale observations and the way an action is parameterised did more damage than any modelling choice I made.

02

From a handheld demonstration to a training set

Demonstrations are collected with a handheld gripper rather than by moving the robot, which is faster and does not need the robot present. The cost is that every demonstration has to be reconstructed into robot terms before anything can learn from it.

What we throw away, and why

The pose of the handheld gripper comes from visual tracking, and tracking fails. A dropped frame is obvious. A frame where tracking has drifted is not, and it trains the policy to associate an image with a pose the gripper was never at.

So validation runs per step rather than per episode, and a step that fails is dropped. Dropping data feels wrong the first few times. It is much cheaper than finding out later that a policy learned from poses that never happened.

03

Changing the model, and having to justify it

The stack ran on a 171-million-parameter U-Net diffusion policy. It now runs on a 3-billion-parameter vision-language-action model. The reason was not that bigger is better. It was that the diffusion policy did not generalise outside the distribution it was trained on, and I could show that.

How I argued for it

Not with a benchmark score. A change of that size needs a reason somebody else can check, so I wrote it up as a cited review of published handover and out-of-distribution results and put my own measurements next to them. The argument was that the failure was a generalisation failure rather than a capacity or tuning failure, and that scaling the same architecture would not fix it.

04

Closing the loop on real hardware

Inference takes about 125 milliseconds on the GPU. The arm wants a command every millisecond. Those two numbers do not meet, and everything in this panel exists to bridge them.

The part that is not modelling at all

A workspace gate on the client, because a policy that has never seen the edge of the table will happily drive into it. Rate limiting, because a chunk boundary can ask for a step the arm should not take in one cycle. And impedance control rather than position control underneath, so contact is survivable instead of a fault.

The gap between training and deployment

This is the panel I would show first. Three defects, all in the deployment client rather than the model, each one individually plausible and quiet. Turn them on and watch what they do to the same policy.

Every one of these looked like a model problem when I first met it. That is what made them expensive.

What each one does

How long each of these took to find

The frame rotation was the worst, because a consistent rotation looks like a policy that has learned a slightly wrong grasp approach. I went looking in the model first, retrained, and got the same behaviour back.

The stale observations were subtler again. The policy was acting on what the camera saw a control cycle or two ago, which at low speed is almost invisible and at speed turns into overshoot.

The observation mask removed 5.6 percent of the image the model was trained on. Small enough that nothing looked broken, large enough that the policy was being asked to work from a different picture than it learned from.

06

Video compression as domain shift

The demonstrations are video. Video gets compressed. The policy trains on the compressed frames and then runs on frames from a live camera, which are not compressed the same way, and the difference is a domain shift nobody wrote down.

Why this was worth doing at all

It was not an obvious place to look. I only went there because the evaluation had become trustworthy enough to show a difference this size, which is the previous panel and the one after it doing their job.

07

One rollout tells you almost nothing

A diffusion policy samples. Run the same policy on the same episode twice and you get two different trajectories. Compare two policies on one rollout each and you are mostly comparing two samples.

What I was doing before

Comparing single trajectories and believing the result. It is fast and it feels like measurement. Establishing that it could not distinguish a real improvement from sampler variance is what made the compression work possible, because until then there was no instrument fine enough to see the improvement it produced.

08

Do quantum layers buy parameter efficiency

A separate question inside the same stack. If parameterised quantum circuits replaced the attention projections in a vision transformer policy, would accuracy hold at fewer parameters. The comparison was pre-registered, with matched classical controls on identical episode splits, a width ladder spanning a 13 times parameter range, three seeds per configuration, and an acceptance criterion fixed before data collection and enforced in code.

The finding that nearly invalidated the experiment

An early pass had the patch-embedding convolutions taking 89 percent of the parameter count at the narrowest width, which left the quantum layers at 1.4 percent of the model. Any efficiency claim would have been a claim about the classical layers around them.

Restructuring the architecture raised the quantum share to 10.5 percent. That is the difference between measuring the thing you said you were measuring and measuring something else with the same name.

09

What is next

Roughly the order I would pick it up.

  1. Whether the quantum arm degrades more slowly below the capacity knee

    At matched parameter count the two arms are indistinguishable. The open question is what happens when you keep narrowing, where the classical arm should fall away first if the efficiency claim has anything in it. That is a longer ladder and more seeds rather than a new idea.

  2. Handover on two arms rather than one

    Everything on this page is one arm reaching for a static object. Handover is two policies whose errors compound, and the interesting failure is the one where both are individually fine.

  3. Evaluation that survives a reviewer

    Episode-level splits and repeated rollouts got the measurements trustworthy enough to work with. Making them defensible in a paper needs a pre-registered protocol and a power calculation rather than my judgement about how many rollouts is enough.

  4. Compute budgets that match the deployment

    A 3-billion-parameter model at 125 milliseconds is fine on a workstation GPU and is not a spacecraft power budget. If the quantum work goes anywhere useful it goes here, into the projection modules that run once per inference rather than inside the iterative denoising loop where a 140 times multiplier would be untenable.
