In short

My research is robot learning for in-space servicing, assembly and manufacturing. I work on imitation learning policies, diffusion policies and vision-language-action models, trained from human demonstrations and run on a Franka FR3. The hard part is rarely the architecture. It is the gap between a policy that scores well offline and one that still closes on the hardware under tight power and compute budgets, communication latency, and nobody in the loop to correct a bad grasp. Most of that gap lives in frame conventions, stale observations, how the action is parameterized, and how the evaluation is split.

Alongside that I built the market data platform at EQ Risk Management Consulting, from live tick ingestion through to backtest. It is in daily production use by a trader with twenty years in institutional index options.

What connects them is evaluation. I am most useful on problems where the result is easy to fake and hard to verify, and most of my work on both sides has been making a measurement trustworthy before anything gets built on top of it. That is not glamorous work and it is usually the difference between a demo and a system. On the robotics side it took held-out error from six times the grasp tolerance to inside it. On the trading side it retired 13 of 14 candidate signals.

Current research

Quantum vision transformer policies for robotic manipulation

Can parameterized quantum circuits replace the attention projections in a vision transformer policy, and hold accuracy at fewer parameters? Implemented in PennyLane and PyTorch, benchmarked on a Franka Emika FR3 reach-and-grasp task.

Most of the work so far has been making the question answerable. An existing model family turned out to have been trained against the wrong target, regressing the robot’s absolute pose at the current timestep rather than a future action. That makes a pose estimator, not a controller. It had also been evaluated on a frame-level random split that leaked temporally adjacent frames between train and validation. Held-out error was 192 mm against a 30 mm grasp tolerance.

Rebuilding the pipeline around relative action targets in the gripper frame, episode-level splits, and mean-predictor baselines reported as R² took held-out R² from approximately zero to +0.455 across 222 demonstrations, covering 293,000 frames and 88,000 training samples, and brought error from 192 mm down to roughly 18 mm.

On that footing the comparison is pre-registered. Quantum and matched classical controls run on identical episode splits, across a width ladder spanning a 13× parameter range, three seeds per configuration, with an acceptance criterion fixed before data collection and enforced in code. An early pass showed patch-embedding convolutions dominating the parameter count at 89 percent of the narrowest width, which left the quantum layers at 1.4 percent. Restructuring the architecture raised the quantum share to 10.5 percent, so the efficiency claim is about the quantum component rather than the classical layers around it.

The current finding is that quantum and classical attention are statistically indistinguishable at matched parameter count, at roughly 140× the wall-clock cost in simulation. The open question is whether the quantum arm degrades more slowly below the capacity knee.

This is a parameter-efficiency question inside the imitation learning stack rather than a separate line of work. If quantum layers do preserve accuracy at fewer parameters, where they would go is the embodiment-specific state and action projection modules of a vision-language-action model, which run once per inference rather than inside the iterative denoising loop, where a 140× multiplier would be untenable in a control loop.

Handover that survives contact with the real world

Two independent robots passing an object between them, in orbit, with no one in the loop to correct a bad grasp. A policy that does it in the lab and fails on the hardware has learned the lab. Most of my time on the manipulation stack has gone not into the model but into the gap between training and deployment. Frame conventions, stale observations, compression artifacts and masked pixels all sit in that gap, as does the work of building evaluation strong enough to tell a real improvement from sampler variance. In-space servicing, assembly and manufacturing sets the constraints, among them tight power and compute budgets, communication latency, and very little opportunity to collect or validate data. The first step, reproducing the demonstration-to-policy pipeline on commodity hardware and verifying it against a Franka arm, is published at NAECON 2026.

Graduate Research Assistant

University of Cincinnati · Aug 2025 – present

Robot learning for in-space servicing, assembly and manufacturing, built around the problem of handing an object from one independent robot to another, reliably, outside the conditions it was trained in.

  • Building a control framework for robotic handover from imitation learning and vision-language-action models.
  • Migrated the manipulation stack from a 171M-parameter U-Net diffusion policy to NVIDIA GR00T N1.7, a 3B-parameter VLA, after diagnosing that the diffusion policy would not generalize outside its training distribution.
  • Built the conversion pipeline from Universal Manipulation Interface recordings to LeRobot v2.1, covering absolute end-effector pose actions, language annotations and modality metadata across 265,000 frames and 215 demonstrations, with per-step validation that rejects SLAM tracking failures.
  • Deployed it as a real-time closed-loop controller with 125 ms GPU inference served over ZMQ, action chunks subsampled and rate-limited to a workspace-gated client, and execution through Cartesian impedance control at 1 kHz on a Franka FR3.
  • Found and corrected systematic train-deploy mismatches, including a 45-degree tool-frame rotation error, stale camera frames buffered across control cycles, and an observation mask that removed 5.6 percent of the image the model was trained on.
  • Traced a further source of domain shift to video compression in the data pipeline, then rebuilt the conversion at 3× fidelity and retrained, cutting prediction error against the original model on 20 held-out episodes at every checkpoint. That followed from first establishing that single-trajectory evaluation could not separate real improvement from sampler variance.
  • Built the collection pipeline itself, using a UMI gripper with GoPro cameras and ArUco fiducials, with video processed through ORB-SLAM3 to recover 6-DOF gripper poses synchronized with RGB observations and gripper state.
  • Exploring quantum-inspired low-rank attention to compress the vision encoder for resource-constrained space-grade hardware.

Publications

From Human Demonstrations to Robotic Actions: Real2Sim Verification of a Diffusion Policy for Autonomous Robotic Manipulation

Neal D’Andrea, Joshua Wachs, Abdou Wade, Ou Ma, Raj Bhatnagar · NAECON 2026, IEEE National Aerospace and Electronics Conference · August 2026 · pp. 402–407

The Universal Manipulation Interface trains manipulation policies from handheld human demonstrations, and works well for robots on the ground. Space is a harder case. Harsh conditions, tight power and compute budgets, communication latency, and very little opportunity to collect or validate data all work against it. This paper is the first step toward carrying the approach across, with a comparable gripper built and tested in both simulation and experiment on a Franka Emika arm, reproducing the data collection and diffusion policy training pipeline on commodity hardware.
